## Why Sidewalk Safety Matters

Every year, thousands of pedestrians are injured or killed in traffic-related incidents, while countless others experience safety concerns ranging from harassment to assault. According to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, a pedestrian is killed every 81 minutes in traffic crashes in the United States. Beyond traffic dangers, walking alone—especially during early morning or evening hours—can present personal safety challenges that require awareness and preparation.

The good news? Most sidewalk accidents and incidents are preventable with the right knowledge, awareness, and safety tools. By implementing smart walking habits and leveraging safety technology, you can significantly reduce your risk while enjoying the benefits of walking.

## Stay Aware of Your Surroundings

The foundation of sidewalk safety begins with situational awareness. Many pedestrians fall into the trap of walking on autopilot, buried in their phones or lost in thought, which makes them vulnerable to both accidents and threats.

### Remove Distractions

While it's tempting to scroll through social media or respond to texts while walking, doing so severely compromises your awareness. If you must use your phone, stop in a safe location rather than walking and texting simultaneously. When listening to music or podcasts, keep the volume low enough to hear approaching vehicles, cyclists, or people around you—or better yet, use only one earbud so one ear remains free.

### Scan Your Environment Regularly

Develop the habit of regularly scanning your surroundings—ahead, behind, and to your sides. Notice the people around you, identify potential exit routes, and stay alert to changes in your environment. Trust your instincts; if something feels off about a person, place, or situation, take action by crossing the street, entering a public business, or using a safety app to alert your contacts.

## Follow Traffic Rules and Signals

Pedestrians must share the road responsibly with vehicles, and that means following the same traffic signals and rules designed to keep everyone safe.

### Use Crosswalks and Obey Signals

Always cross streets at designated crosswalks when available, and wait for the walk signal even if the street appears clear. Drivers expect pedestrians at marked crossings, making these locations significantly safer than jaywalking. When crossing, make eye contact with drivers to ensure they see you before stepping into the street.

### Look Both Ways—Always

It sounds elementary, but looking left, right, and left again before crossing can save your life. Don't assume drivers will stop just because you have the right of way. Be especially cautious at intersections where vehicles may be turning, as drivers often focus on oncoming traffic rather than pedestrians.

## Walk Facing Traffic When No Sidewalk Exists

In areas without sidewalks, your positioning can make a significant difference in your safety. Always walk facing oncoming traffic (on the left side of the road in countries where people drive on the right). This allows you to see vehicles approaching and gives you time to move out of the way if necessary.

Wear bright or reflective clothing when walking during low-light conditions, and consider carrying a flashlight or wearing a headlamp to increase your visibility to drivers.

## Plan Your Route Strategically

Not all routes are created equal when it comes to safety. Taking a few minutes to plan your walking route can dramatically reduce your risk.

### Choose Well-Lit, Populated Paths

Whenever possible, stick to well-lit streets with regular foot traffic. Avoid shortcuts through dark alleys, parking structures, or isolated areas, especially during early morning or evening hours. The few minutes you might save aren't worth the increased risk.

### Vary Your Routine

If you walk the same route regularly, such as a daily commute, consider occasionally varying your path and timing. Predictable patterns can make you a target for criminals who observe routines. Changing things up keeps potential threats uncertain about your schedule.

## Be Cautious at Intersections and Driveways

Intersections and driveways are high-risk areas where pedestrians and vehicles converge. Exercise extra caution in these zones.

### Watch for Turning Vehicles

Drivers making turns often focus on traffic coming from one direction and may not notice pedestrians. Never assume a driver sees you, even if you're in a crosswalk. Be particularly wary of vehicles turning right on red lights, as drivers may be looking left for oncoming traffic while you approach from their right.

### Check Driveways and Parking Lot Exits

Vehicles backing out of driveways or parking spaces pose a significant threat to pedestrians. Always look for backup lights or movement behind parked cars, and give vehicles plenty of space. Walk behind cars that are backing up rather than trying to hurry past them.

## Maintain Personal Safety Awareness

Beyond traffic dangers, pedestrians must also be aware of personal safety threats. While most walks are uneventful, being prepared for potential confrontations or emergencies is essential.

### Keep Valuables Concealed

Don't advertise expensive electronics, jewelry, or cash while walking. Keep your phone in your pocket rather than in your hand when possible, and wear bags across your body rather than dangling from one shoulder, making them harder to snatch.

### Trust Your Instincts

Your intuition is a powerful safety tool. If someone makes you uncomfortable or you feel unsafe in a particular area, take immediate action. Cross the street, enter a nearby business, or head toward areas with more people. Don't worry about appearing rude—your safety is more important than social niceties.

## Walk Confidently and Purposefully

Your body language and demeanor while walking can influence how others perceive you. Walking confidently with your head up, shoulders back, and purposeful stride sends the message that you're alert and not an easy target.

Avoid appearing lost or confused, even if you are. If you need to check directions, step into a business or against a building rather than stopping in the middle of the sidewalk looking at your phone. This reduces your vulnerability and keeps you from blocking foot traffic.

## Be Prepared for Weather and Environmental Hazards

Sidewalk safety isn't just about traffic and personal security—environmental hazards pose significant risks as well.

### Watch for Slippery Surfaces

Rain, ice, snow, and even wet leaves can make sidewalks treacherous. Slow down in these conditions, wear appropriate footwear with good traction, and test surfaces before putting your full weight down. Many pedestrian injuries occur from simple slips and falls that could have been prevented with extra caution.

### Navigate Construction Zones Carefully

Construction sites, uneven pavement, potholes, and debris can all create tripping hazards. Stay alert for warning signs, and don't be afraid to cross the street if a sidewalk appears unsafe. If you must walk through a construction zone, proceed slowly and watch for both ground hazards and overhead dangers.

There's safety in numbers. Whenever possible, walk with friends, family, or coworkers, especially during early morning or evening hours. If you typically walk alone, consider joining or forming a walking group with neighbors or coworkers who travel similar routes.

When walking in groups, stay together and remain aware of each other. Designate someone to lead and someone to watch the back of the group, ensuring no one falls behind or becomes separated.
