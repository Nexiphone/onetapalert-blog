## Understanding Workplace Harassment

Workplace harassment encompasses any unwelcome conduct based on protected characteristics such as race, gender, age, disability, religion, or sexual orientation. It also includes bullying, threats, intimidation, and any behavior that creates a hostile work environment.

### Types of Workplace Harassment

**Verbal Harassment**: Offensive jokes, slurs, name-calling, threats, or intimidating language directed at you or about your protected characteristics.

**Physical Harassment**: Unwanted touching, blocking your movement, physical intimidation, assault, or threatening gestures.

**Visual Harassment**: Offensive images, gestures, or written materials that create a hostile environment.

**Sexual Harassment**: Unwanted sexual advances, requests for sexual favors, inappropriate touching, or sexually explicit comments.

**Cyber Harassment**: Threatening emails, messages, or social media posts from colleagues or supervisors.

Understanding these categories helps you identify harassment when it occurs and take appropriate action to protect yourself.

## Immediate Safety Strategies During Harassment Incidents

When facing an active harassment situation, your immediate safety is the top priority. Here are critical steps to take:

### Create Physical Distance

If someone is harassing you physically or verbally, remove yourself from the situation immediately if possible. Move to a public area, your supervisor's office, or outside the building if necessary. Trust your instincts—if a situation feels dangerous, it probably is.

### Use Your Voice

Clearly and loudly state "Stop" or "Leave me alone" if it's safe to do so. This establishes a clear boundary and creates witnesses who can corroborate your account later.

## Building a Workplace Safety Plan

### Map Your Workplace Safety Zones

Identify safe areas in your workplace where cameras are present, security is stationed, or other people are typically around. Plan routes to these areas from various parts of your workplace.

Create code words or phrases with your emergency contacts that signal different levels of danger. A phrase like "I'm running late" might mean you need them to call you immediately to create an excuse to leave, while "emergency" means call 911.

## Documenting Workplace Harassment Effectively

Proper documentation is essential for HR complaints, legal action, and protecting your credibility.

### What to Document

Record every incident with these details:
- **Date and time** (be specific: "May 15, 2026, approximately 3:45 PM")
- **Location** (exact room, floor, or area)
- **What happened** (direct quotes if possible, specific actions)
- **Who was involved** (names of harassers and witnesses)
- **Your response** (what you said or did)
- **How it affected you** (emotional distress, physical symptoms, work impact)
- **Who you reported it to** (supervisor, HR, etc.)

### Save All Communications

Screenshot and save all harassing emails, texts, social media messages, and voicemails. Never delete these even if they're upsetting—they're crucial evidence.

## Legal Protections and Reporting Options

Understanding your legal rights empowers you to take appropriate action.

### Federal Protections

Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits workplace harassment based on race, color, religion, sex, and national origin. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) enforces these protections. Other federal laws protect against age, disability, and genetic information discrimination.

### State and Local Protections

Many states and cities offer additional protections beyond federal law. Research your local regulations or consult with an employment attorney.

### Internal Reporting

Most employers require you to report harassment through internal channels first:
1. Report to your immediate supervisor (unless they're the harasser)
2. File a formal complaint with HR
3. Follow your company's harassment reporting procedures
4. Keep copies of all reports and correspondence

### External Reporting

If internal reporting doesn't resolve the situation or if you face retaliation:
- File an EEOC complaint (within 180-300 days of the incident, depending on your state)
- Contact your state's fair employment agency
- Consult an employment attorney
- Report criminal behavior (assault, stalking, threats) to police

## Addressing Retaliation Fears

Many harassment victims fear retaliation for reporting. While retaliation is illegal, it does occur, so take these protective steps:

### Document Everything

Keep detailed records of your job performance, accomplishments, and any changes in treatment after reporting harassment.

### Know the Signs of Retaliation

Watch for sudden negative performance reviews, undesirable assignments, exclusion from meetings, reduction in hours, or termination following your complaint.

### Understand Your Legal Protections

Retaliation for reporting harassment is illegal under federal and most state laws. If you experience retaliation, report it immediately and consult an attorney.

## Self-Care After Workplace Harassment

Harassment takes a significant emotional and physical toll. Prioritize your well-being:

### Seek Professional Support

Consider working with a therapist who specializes in workplace trauma. Many offer virtual sessions that can fit around your work schedule.

### Build Your Support Network

Connect with others who have experienced workplace harassment through support groups or online communities. Sharing experiences reduces isolation and provides practical coping strategies.

### Prioritize Physical Safety

Continue using safety tools like **One Tap Alert** even after harassment has been reported. Harassers may escalate behavior when they face consequences, so maintaining your safety measures is essential.

### Consider Your Options

If your employer fails to address harassment adequately, consider whether remaining in that environment is sustainable. Your safety and mental health are more important than any job.

## Creating a Harassment-Free Workplace Culture

While individual safety measures are essential, systemic change is the ultimate goal.
