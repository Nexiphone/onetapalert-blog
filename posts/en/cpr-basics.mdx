## What is CPR and When Should You Use It?

CPR stands for cardiopulmonary resuscitation—a life-saving technique used when someone's heart stops beating or they stop breathing. By manually pumping the chest and providing rescue breaths, CPR keeps oxygen-rich blood flowing to the brain and other vital organs until professional medical help arrives.

### Recognizing When CPR is Needed

You should perform CPR when someone is:

- **Unresponsive** to touch or voice
- **Not breathing** or only gasping occasionally
- **Showing no signs of circulation** (no pulse, no movement, no coughing)

Time is absolutely critical in these situations. For every minute that passes without CPR, a person's chance of survival decreases by 7-10%. CPR may be necessary in various scenarios:

- Heart attacks
- Drowning incidents
- Severe allergic reactions
- Electrocution
- Drug overdoses
- Choking (if the person becomes unconscious)
- Severe trauma or bleeding

## The CPR Steps: A Complete Walkthrough

Before we dive into the technique, remember the most important rule: **any CPR is better than no CPR**. Even if you're not perfectly trained, your actions can still save a life.

### Step 1: Assess the Situation and Call for Help

First, ensure the scene is safe for both you and the victim. Then:

1. **Check for responsiveness**: Tap the person's shoulder and shout, "Are you okay?"
2. **Call 911 immediately** (or have someone else call)

### Step 2: Position the Person Correctly

- Lay the person flat on their back on a firm surface
- Kneel beside their chest
- Clear any obvious obstructions from the airway (remove visible objects from the mouth)

### Step 3: Perform Chest Compressions

This is the most critical component of CPR:

1. **Hand placement**: Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest (on the lower half of the breastbone), then place your other hand on top
2. **Body position**: Position your shoulders directly over your hands and keep your arms straight
3. **Compression depth**: Push hard and fast, compressing the chest at least 2 inches (5 cm) deep
4. **Compression rate**: Aim for 100-120 compressions per minute (about the tempo of the Bee Gees song "Stayin' Alive")
5. **Allow full recoil**: Let the chest come back up completely between compressions

### Step 4: Give Rescue Breaths (If Trained)

If you're trained in CPR and comfortable giving rescue breaths:

1. Tilt the person's head back slightly to open the airway
2. Pinch the nose shut
3. Make a seal over the person's mouth with yours
4. Give two breaths (each lasting about one second), watching for the chest to rise
5. Continue the cycle: 30 compressions followed by 2 breaths

**Important note**: If you're not trained or uncomfortable giving rescue breaths, hands-only CPR (continuous chest compressions) is still highly effective, especially for adults.

### Step 5: Continue Until Help Arrives

Keep performing CPR until:

- Emergency medical services arrive and take over
- The person shows signs of life (breathing, movement, consciousness)
- You're too exhausted to continue
- An AED becomes available

## Hands-Only CPR: Simpler and Still Effective

The American Heart Association now recommends that untrained bystanders perform hands-only CPR for adult cardiac arrest victims. This simplified approach:

- Eliminates the need for rescue breaths
- Focuses solely on continuous, high-quality chest compressions
- Is easier to remember and perform under stress
- Has been shown to be just as effective as traditional CPR in many cases

The steps are simple:
1. Call 911 (and activate **One Tap Alert**)
2. Push hard and fast in the center of the chest at 100-120 compressions per minute
3. Don't stop until help arrives

## CPR for Different Age Groups

The basic principles of CPR remain the same, but there are important differences based on the victim's age.

### Adult CPR (Over 8 Years Old)

- Use both hands for compressions
- Compress at least 2 inches deep
- Use the standard 30:2 ratio (compressions to breaths)

### Child CPR (1-8 Years Old)

- Use one or two hands for compressions (depending on the child's size)
- Compress about 2 inches deep (one-third the depth of the chest)
- Use the 30:2 ratio
- Be slightly gentler than with adults, but still firm and effective

### Infant CPR (Under 1 Year Old)

- Use two fingers for compressions
- Compress about 1.5 inches deep (one-third the depth of the chest)
- Use the 30:2 ratio
- Cover both the infant's mouth and nose with your mouth when giving rescue breaths

## Common CPR Mistakes to Avoid

Even well-intentioned rescuers can make errors during CPR. Here are the most common mistakes:

### Not Pushing Hard Enough

Many people are afraid of hurting the victim, so they don't compress deeply enough. Remember: broken ribs heal, but death is permanent. Push hard and don't be afraid to use your full body weight.

### Stopping Too Often

Interruptions in chest compressions reduce the effectiveness of CPR dramatically. Minimize pauses and keep your compressions continuous.

### Poor Hand Placement

Hands placed too high or too low on the chest won't be as effective. Always aim for the center of the chest, on the lower half of the breastbone.

### Not Calling for Help First

Never skip the step of calling 911 and alerting others. This is where **One Tap Alert** becomes invaluable—you can notify unlimited emergency contacts instantly, creating a network of support while you focus on the physical aspects of rescue.

### Giving Up Too Soon

CPR is exhausting, but you should continue until professional help arrives. If possible, switch with another capable person every two minutes to maintain compression quality.
