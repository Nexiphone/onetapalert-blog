## What Is Phishing and Why It Threatens Your Safety

Phishing is a cybercrime where attackers impersonate legitimate organizations or individuals through email, text, or social media to trick you into revealing sensitive information. Unlike traditional scams, modern phishing attempts can be incredibly sophisticated, often indistinguishable from authentic communications at first glance.

The danger extends beyond financial loss. Phishing attacks can compromise:

- **Personal safety information**: Home addresses, daily routines, and location data
- **Identity documents**: Social Security numbers, driver's licenses, and passports
- **Access to security systems**: Smart home devices, security cameras, and alarm systems
- **Emergency contact information**: Names and contact details of trusted individuals

When criminals gain access to your personal safety information, they can track your movements, identify when you're home alone, or even impersonate you to target your loved ones. ## 12 Warning Signs of Phishing Emails

### 1. Urgent or Threatening Language

Phishing emails create artificial urgency to bypass your critical thinking. Watch for phrases like:

- "Your account will be closed in 24 hours"
- "Immediate action required"
- "Suspicious activity detected—verify now"
- "Final warning"

Legitimate organizations rarely demand instant action without proper notice. If you receive an urgent email, verify it through official channels before responding. Just as **One Tap Alert's Safety Timer** gives you a secure way to check in during potentially risky situations, taking a moment to verify suspicious emails can prevent a digital emergency.

### 2. Generic Greetings

Authentic companies that have your information will address you by name. Phishing emails often use:

- "Dear Customer"
- "Valued User"
- "Account Holder"
- "Sir/Madam"

If your bank, healthcare provider, or any service you use doesn't address you personally, it's a red flag.

### 3. Suspicious Sender Email Addresses

Always examine the sender's email address carefully. Phishing attempts often use addresses that look legitimate at first glance:

- support@amaz0n-security.com (note the zero)
- accounts@paypa1.services (note the number 1)
- alert@bankofamerica.updates.ru (suspicious domain)

Hover over the sender's name (without clicking) to reveal the actual email address. Legitimate companies use consistent, official domain names.

### 4. Spelling and Grammar Mistakes

While not all phishing emails contain obvious errors, many still do. Professional organizations employ editors and proofreaders. Watch for:

- Awkward phrasing
- Inconsistent formatting
- Misspelled words
- Strange capitalization

These errors often indicate emails sent by non-native speakers or automated phishing tools.

### 5. Suspicious Links and Attachments

Never click links or download attachments from unexpected emails. Phishing links often:

- Use URL shorteners to hide the true destination
- Contain misspelled domain names
- Redirect through multiple sites
- Lead to fake login pages designed to steal credentials

Hover over links (without clicking) to preview the URL. If it looks suspicious or doesn't match the supposed sender's website, don't click.

### 6. Requests for Personal Information

Legitimate organizations will never ask for sensitive information via email:

- Passwords or PINs
- Social Security numbers
- Credit card details with CVV codes
- Account security questions

Just as you'd store your ID, insurance cards, and medical information in **One Tap Alert's Secure Vault** rather than sending photos via text, legitimate companies have secure portals for sensitive data—they'll never request it through email.

### 7. Too-Good-To-Be-True Offers

Phishing emails often promise unrealistic rewards:

- "You've won a prize" (from contests you didn't enter)
- "Congratulations! You've been selected for a $1,000 gift card"
- "Exclusive offer—99% discount"
- "Claim your inheritance from a distant relative"

If it sounds too good to be true, it almost certainly is.

### 8. Mismatched or Suspicious Branding

Examine logos, colors, and formatting carefully. Phishing emails often feature:

- Low-resolution or distorted logos
- Incorrect brand colors
- Poor-quality images
- Inconsistent fonts

Compare the email to legitimate communications from the same organization.

### 9. Unusual Requests or Business Practices

Be skeptical of emails that:

- Ask you to verify information the company should already have
- Request payment through unusual methods (gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers)
- Come from unexpected departments
- Arrive outside normal business communications

### 10. Missing Contact Information

Legitimate business emails include:

- Physical business addresses
- Customer service phone numbers
- Official website links
- Unsubscribe options (for marketing emails)

Phishing emails often lack this information or provide fake contact details.

### 11. Emails About Accounts You Don't Have

This seems obvious, but sophisticated phishing campaigns cast wide nets. You might receive:

- "Your Netflix subscription is expiring" (when you don't use Netflix)
- "Your package couldn't be delivered" (when you're not expecting anything)
- "Your PayPal account is locked" (when you don't have PayPal)

These mass phishing attempts rely on the law of large numbers—some recipients will have these accounts.

### 12. Unexpected Password Reset or Security Alerts

If you receive password reset confirmations or security alerts you didn't request, someone may be attempting to access your account. Don't click links in these emails. Instead:

- Go directly to the website by typing the URL yourself
- Log in through your normal method
- Change your password immediately if unauthorized access is detected
- Enable two-factor authentication
