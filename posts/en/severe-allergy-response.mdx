## Understanding Severe Allergic Reactions and Anaphylaxis

### What Makes an Allergic Reaction Severe?

Not all allergic reactions are created equal. Mild reactions might include localized hives, itching, or minor swelling. Severe allergic reactions, however, involve multiple body systems and can progress to anaphylaxis—a potentially fatal whole-body response.

**Anaphylaxis symptoms include:**
- Difficulty breathing or wheezing
- Swelling of the throat, tongue, or face
- Rapid or weak pulse
- Severe drop in blood pressure
- Skin reactions (hives, flushed or pale skin)
- Nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea
- Dizziness or fainting
- Sense of impending doom

The most dangerous aspect of anaphylaxis is how quickly it can develop. Some reactions occur within seconds of exposure, while others may take up to several hours. This unpredictability makes preparation absolutely critical.

### Common Triggers to Know

Understanding what triggers severe reactions helps you avoid exposure and recognize emergency situations faster:

- **Foods**: Peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, fish, milk, eggs, wheat, and soy account for 90% of food allergies
- **Insect stings**: Bees, wasps, hornets, yellow jackets, and fire ants
- **Medications**: Penicillin, aspirin, and other common drugs
- **Latex**: Medical gloves, balloons, and rubber products
- **Exercise**: In rare cases, physical activity can trigger anaphylaxis (exercise-induced anaphylaxis)

## The Critical First 5 Minutes: Your Emergency Response Plan

When someone experiences a severe allergic reaction, the first five minutes determine the outcome. Here's your step-by-step action plan.

### Step 1: Recognize the Signs Immediately

Don't wait to see if symptoms improve. If you or someone around you has known allergies and develops any of the following after exposure, treat it as anaphylaxis:

- Any difficulty breathing
- Swelling affecting the airway
- Multiple symptoms across different body systems
- Symptoms that rapidly worsen

Trust your instincts. It's always better to overreact to a mild situation than underreact to a severe one.

### Step 2: Administer Epinephrine Without Delay

If an epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen, Auvi-Q, or generic version) is available, use it immediately. This is not the time to hesitate or "wait and see."

**Proper epinephrine administration:**
1. Remove the auto-injector from its carrier tube
2. Hold it firmly with the orange tip pointing downward
3. Remove the blue safety cap by pulling straight up
4. Place the orange tip against the outer thigh (can go through clothing)
5. Push down hard until you hear a click
6. Hold firmly in place for 3 seconds
7. Remove and massage the injection area for 10 seconds

After administering epinephrine, the person should lie down with legs elevated (unless they're vomiting or having trouble breathing, in which case they should sit up). Keep them still and calm.

### Step 4: Prepare for a Possible Second Dose

About 20-30% of severe allergic reactions require a second dose of epinephrine. If symptoms don't improve after 5-10 minutes, or if they return after initial improvement, administer a second dose if available. This is why allergists recommend carrying two auto-injectors at all times.

### Always Carry Two EpiPens

Medical guidelines recommend carrying two epinephrine auto-injectors at all times because:
- About 20-30% of severe reactions require a second dose
- Auto-injectors can malfunction
- The first dose might be dropped or lost during the emergency
- You might have a reaction while away from your primary device

Keep one on your person and one in an easily accessible location. Add reminders to your phone to check expiration dates quarterly—expired epinephrine loses effectiveness.

### Wear Medical Identification

A medical alert bracelet or necklace provides crucial information when you can't speak. It should include:
- "Anaphylaxis" or "Severe Allergies"
- Specific allergens
- "Carries EpiPen"
- Emergency contact number

While **One Tap Alert** notifies your contacts, a medical ID ensures first responders and bystanders who aren't in your network can also help appropriately.

## Recognizing Delayed and Biphasic Reactions

Not all severe allergic reactions happen immediately. Understanding delayed and biphasic reactions can prevent dangerous assumptions.

### Delayed Reactions

Some allergic reactions develop hours after exposure. This is particularly common with:
- Certain medications
- Some food proteins
- Latex allergies

If you know you've been exposed to an allergen, remain vigilant for 4-6 hours afterward. Consider setting a **One Tap Alert Safety Timer** for several hours post-exposure, especially if you'll be alone.

### Biphasic Reactions

In 1-20% of anaphylaxis cases, symptoms return after the initial reaction has resolved—sometimes up to 72 hours later. This is why:
- You must always go to the emergency room after using an EpiPen
- Hospital observation is often recommended
- You should remain cautious for several days

Even if you feel completely better after using epinephrine, seek emergency medical care. Use **One Tap Alert** to notify your emergency contacts so someone can stay with you during the observation period.
