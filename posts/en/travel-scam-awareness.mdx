## The Most Common Travel Scams in 2026

### The Fake Taxi Scam

One of the oldest tricks in the book, the fake taxi scam has evolved significantly in the digital age. Scammers now use fake ride-sharing apps, counterfeit taxi signs, and sophisticated meters to overcharge unsuspecting tourists. In some cases, drivers may take unnecessarily long routes, while in more dangerous scenarios, they may drive you to isolated areas where accomplices are waiting.

**What to watch for:**
- Taxis without official markings or licenses
- Drivers who refuse to use the meter
- Unusually low prices offered before getting in
- Vehicles parked suspiciously close to tourist areas rather than official taxi stands

### The "Helpful" Local Scam

This scam preys on travelers' desire for authentic local experiences. A friendly stranger approaches offering unsolicited help with directions, restaurant recommendations, or tourist attractions. They may take you to their "family's shop," a restaurant where they receive commission, or even to secluded areas where theft or assault can occur.

While most locals genuinely want to help, scammers exploit this goodwill. They're often well-dressed, speak excellent English, and seem trustworthy—making them particularly effective at catching travelers off guard.

**Red flags include:**
- Overly persistent offers of help when you haven't asked for it
- Insistence on taking you somewhere specific
- Stories that seem too good to be true (free tours, exclusive access, etc.)
- Requests for you to follow them away from crowded areas

### The Distraction Theft Scam

This sophisticated operation typically involves a team of thieves. One person creates a distraction—spilling something on you, asking for directions with a map, or staging a commotion—while accomplices steal your belongings. Common variations include the "baby toss" (handing you a baby or doll while they rob you), the "bird poop" scam (spraying fake bird droppings on you then "helping" clean it while pickpocketing), and coordinated ATM thefts.

These scams are prevalent in tourist-heavy areas like popular squares, metro stations, and major attractions. The key is that your attention is completely diverted, giving thieves precious seconds to operate.

### The Fake Police Officer Scam

In this particularly troubling scam, individuals posing as police officers approach tourists, claim there's been a problem (counterfeit money, drugs, illegal activity), and demand to see your wallet, passport, or belongings for "inspection." During this "inspection," they steal cash, credit cards, or valuable items. In some variations, they'll demand immediate payment of a fine.

This scam is especially dangerous because it exploits travelers' fear of authority and legal trouble in foreign countries. Real police in most countries rarely conduct street inspections of tourists' wallets and will direct you to a police station for any legitimate concerns.

### The Accommodation Scam

With the rise of online booking platforms, accommodation scams have become increasingly sophisticated. Fake listings with stolen photos, properties that don't exist, or "representatives" who meet you with keys to the wrong (or occupied) property are all common variations. Some scammers create nearly perfect replicas of legitimate booking sites to steal your payment information.

Other times, the property exists but looks nothing like the photos, lacks promised amenities, or is in a dangerous neighborhood—information deliberately hidden during booking.

## How to Spot a Travel Scam Before It's Too Late

### Trust Your Instincts

Your gut feeling is often your first line of defense. If something feels off—a deal seems too good, someone is overly pushy, or a situation makes you uncomfortable—trust that instinct. Scammers rely on creating pressure situations where you feel rushed to make decisions without proper consideration.

**One Tap Alert**'s instant SOS button becomes invaluable in these moments. If a situation escalates from uncomfortable to threatening, a simple press and hold for one second alerts all your emergency contacts with your exact location, bringing help when you need it most.

### Research Common Scams for Your Destination

Different regions have signature scams. Before traveling, spend 30 minutes researching common scams specific to your destination. Travel forums, embassy websites, and recent traveler reviews often provide up-to-date information about current scam operations in the area.

Create a safety plan that includes emergency contact numbers saved in **One Tap Alert**. The app allows unlimited emergency contacts, so you can include family members back home, travel companions, your hotel concierge, and even the local embassy or consulate.

### Verify Everything Independently

Never rely solely on information provided by strangers, no matter how helpful they seem. Use your own phone to look up restaurant reviews, attraction prices, and directions. If someone offers you a service, verify the price through official sources first.

For accommodation, always book through verified platforms and verify the property through multiple sources. Call the hotel directly using a number from their official website, not one provided in an email, to confirm reservations.

### Look for Official Credentials

Legitimate service providers have licenses, uniforms with proper identification, and official receipts. Restaurants have physical menus with prices clearly listed. Tour operators have registered businesses with reviews you can verify. Take a moment to examine these credentials before committing to any service.
