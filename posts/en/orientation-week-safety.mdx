## Understanding Orientation Week Risks

Orientation week presents unique safety challenges that differ from the regular academic year. Campus is filled with thousands of unfamiliar faces, established students may take advantage of newcomers' naivety, and the pressure to fit in can lead to risky decisions.

### Common Safety Concerns During Orientation

New students face several specific vulnerabilities during orientation week. The campus is more crowded than usual, with visiting families, prospective students, and returning upperclassmen all converging simultaneously. You're unfamiliar with safe routes, don't know which areas to avoid after dark, and haven't yet established a trusted friend group.

Social pressure peaks during orientation. There's an unspoken urgency to make friends quickly, which can lead students to attend parties with strangers, accept rides from people they've just met, or consume alcohol in unfamiliar settings. Perpetrators of theft, assault, and other crimes specifically target orientation week because they know freshmen are vulnerable and disoriented.

Additionally, you're learning to manage complete independence for potentially the first time. Without parents or guardians nearby, you're responsible for all your decisions—including safety decisions that might previously have been made for you.

## Navigate Social Events Safely

Orientation week typically includes numerous social events—ice cream socials, dorm mixers, club fairs, and unofficial parties. While these events are important for building your social network, they also present safety challenges.

### The Buddy System Still Works

Never underestimate the power of the buddy system. Even if you've just met someone at orientation, partnering up for parties and events significantly improves both people's safety. Agree to arrive together, stay in visual contact, and leave together.

Before heading to any event, share your plans with your roommate or emergency contacts. Let them know where you're going, who you're with, and when you expect to return. Trust your instincts during orientation week. If a situation feels uncomfortable—whether it's a pushy upperclassman, a party in an isolated location, or pressure to consume substances—remove yourself immediately. Your gut reaction is often your best safety tool.

Be especially cautious about alcohol during orientation week. Many campuses see a spike in alcohol-related emergencies during this period. ## Dorm and Housing Safety

Your residence hall is your home base during orientation week and throughout your college career. Establishing good security habits from day one protects both you and your belongings.

### Secure Your Space

Always lock your door, even if you're just running down the hall to the bathroom or visiting a neighbor. Dorm thefts spike during orientation week when doors are frequently propped open and students haven't yet established security routines.

Don't prop residence hall entrance doors open for strangers, even if they claim to be visiting another student. Every legitimate resident has their own key or access card. This simple boundary prevents unauthorized individuals from entering your building.

### Document Your Valuables

Take photos of expensive items like laptops, phones, tablets, and bikes, including serial numbers if possible. Store this documentation, along with copies of your important documents (ID, insurance cards, passport, medical information) in a secure location.

### Know Your Dorm's Safety Resources

Identify your resident advisor (RA) immediately and understand their role in emergency situations. Learn where fire exits are located and what your building's emergency procedures entail. Most residence halls conduct fire drills during orientation week—take these seriously and use them as practice for real emergencies.

## Walking and Transportation Safety

Getting around campus and the surrounding area safely is a critical skill you'll use throughout college, so establishing good habits during orientation week sets the foundation for four years of safe navigation.

### Walking Safety Strategies

Walk confidently and purposefully, even if you're lost. Project awareness of your surroundings by keeping your head up and making brief eye contact with others. Avoid looking at your phone while walking, which signals distraction and vulnerability.

Whenever possible, walk in groups, especially after dark. Most campuses offer evening escort services or shuttle buses—learn these resources during orientation and don't hesitate to use them. There's no prize for walking alone at night, and using these services doesn't make you paranoid or weak.

For late-night walks between locations, use the **One Tap Alert safety timer** to create automatic accountability. This simple step means someone will know if something goes wrong during your walk across campus.

### Transportation Safety

If you use rideshare services during orientation week, verify the driver's identity, license plate, and car model before getting in. Share your trip details with emergency contacts. Sit in the back seat, and trust your instincts—if something feels wrong, cancel the ride.

For students with cars on campus, park in well-lit, designated areas. Check your surroundings before approaching your vehicle, and have your keys ready before you reach the car. If you notice someone suspicious, walk to a populated area or campus police station instead of approaching your vehicle.

## Digital Safety During Orientation Week

Your online presence and digital habits also impact your physical safety during orientation week. New students often overshare on social media or connect with strangers online, creating vulnerabilities.

### Social Media Safety

Avoid posting real-time location updates during orientation week. Posting that you're at a specific party or event broadcasts your location to everyone, including potential threats. Instead, share your experiences after you've left and returned safely.

Be cautious about accepting friend requests or follows from people you haven't met in person. Some predators use orientation week to connect with vulnerable students online before attempting to meet them on campus.

Review your privacy settings on all social media platforms before arriving on campus. Ensure your address, phone number, and detailed location information aren't publicly visible.

### Phone Security

Keep your phone charged at all times during orientation week. Carry a portable charger to events and activities. A dead phone means you can't call for help, use campus maps, or access safety apps like **One Tap Alert** when you need them most.

## Campus Resources Every Freshman Should Know

Your college provides numerous safety resources that are free and available 24/7. Learning about these during orientation week ensures you can access them throughout your college career.

### Campus Police and Security

Locate the campus police station during your first days on campus. Program the campus police number into your phone (it's often different from 911). Learn what situations warrant calling campus police versus local law enforcement.

Many campus police departments offer personal safety presentations during orientation week—attend these even if it seems boring or unnecessary. The information could save your life or prevent an assault.

### Counseling and Health Services

Know where student health services and counseling centers are located. Many students experience anxiety, homesickness, or mental health challenges during orientation week as they adjust to independence. These services exist to support you and are typically free or low-cost for students.

### Title IX Office

Familiarize yourself with your school's Title IX office, which handles reports of sexual harassment, assault, and discrimination. Understanding your rights and available resources before you need them empowers you to take action if something happens.
