## Understanding Ocean Hazards Before You Paddle Out

The ocean is an unpredictable environment that demands respect. Before you even wax your board, understanding the hazards you might face is crucial for your safety.

### Rip Currents: The Silent Threat

Rip currents account for over 80% of beach rescues and claim approximately 100 lives annually in the United States alone. These powerful, narrow channels of fast-moving water flow from shore back out to sea, and they can pull even the strongest swimmers offshore.

Learn to identify rip currents by looking for:
- Discolored or murky water extending from shore
- A line of foam, seaweed, or debris moving steadily seaward
- A break in the incoming wave pattern
- Choppy, turbulent water in a specific area

If caught in a rip current, never swim directly against it. Instead, swim parallel to shore until you're out of the current, then angle back to land. ### Wave Conditions and Swell Size

Understanding swell reports isn't just for finding the best waves—it's critical for safety. Check forecasts for:
- Swell height and interval (time between waves)
- Wind direction and speed
- Tide times and ranges
- Water temperature

Never surf conditions beyond your skill level. A general rule: if the waves are overhead for an intermediate surfer, they're potentially dangerous. ## Pre-Surf Safety Checklist

Preparation is your first line of defense against surfing accidents. Before every session, run through this essential checklist.

### Equipment Inspection

Your surfboard and leash are your primary safety equipment. Check:
- **Leash condition**: No fraying, strong velcro, secure rail saver
- **Leash string**: Not worn or brittle
- **Fins**: Secure and undamaged
- **Deck grip/wax**: Adequate traction
- **Nose guard**: Installed if surfing crowded breaks

A broken leash in big surf can separate you from your flotation device and create a dangerous situation. Replace your leash every season or after any significant stress.

### Physical Preparation

Surfing is physically demanding. Ensure you're ready by:
- Warming up with dynamic stretches
- Staying hydrated before entering the water
- Avoiding surfing when exhausted or under the influence
- Eating adequately beforehand for sustained energy
- Being honest about your fitness level

## In-Water Safety Practices

Once you're in the lineup, maintaining awareness and following ocean etiquette isn't just polite—it's essential for everyone's safety.

### Lineup Positioning and Right-of-Way

Understanding and respecting surf etiquette prevents collisions and injuries:
- The surfer closest to the peak has right-of-way
- Don't drop in on someone already riding a wave
- Don't snake (paddle around someone to get closer to the peak)
- Communicate with other surfers in the lineup
- Apologize if you make a mistake

Surfboard collisions cause lacerations, concussions, and serious injuries. A large percentage of surfing injuries come from being hit by your own or another surfer's board.

### Wipeout Protocol

Knowing how to fall safely reduces injury risk:
- Protect your head with your arms during a wipeout
- Go limp rather than fighting the water
- Wait for turbulence to pass before swimming up
- Look up and around before surfacing to avoid boards
- Don't surface directly where you fell

If you experience a hard wipeout that leaves you disoriented or potentially injured, get to shore and assess your condition. Having **One Tap Alert** already set up on your phone means you can immediately alert your emergency contacts if you realize you're more hurt than initially thought.

### Recognizing When to Call It

Ego has no place in ocean safety. Exit the water if:
- Conditions exceed your skill level
- You're fatigued or cold
- You've had a hard wipeout or collision
- The crowd is aggressive or unsafe
- Weather is deteriorating
- You feel anxious or uncomfortable

## Emergency Response: What to Do When Things Go Wrong

Even with perfect preparation, emergencies happen. Knowing how to respond can save lives.

### Self-Rescue Techniques

If you're in trouble in the water:
1. **Stay calm** and conserve energy
2. **Don't panic** if you lose your board—you can float and swim
3. **Float on your back** if exhausted
4. **Signal for help** by waving one arm overhead
5. **Use your leash** to pull your board back if separated

If you make it to shore but are injured, exhausted, or hypothermic, don't try to tough it out. Use the **One Tap Alert SOS button** to get help immediately, along with your exact coordinates for emergency services.

### Helping Others in Distress

If you see another surfer in trouble:
- Alert the lifeguard immediately if present
- Only attempt a rescue if you're trained and confident
- Never put yourself at equal risk
- Call emergency services (or trigger **One Tap Alert** to notify your contacts who can call)
- Throw a flotation device if available
- Direct the person away from hazards

Your **One Tap Alert** emergency contacts can serve as a communication chain—while you're helping someone in the water, they can be coordinating with emergency services on land.

## Post-Surf Safety Considerations

Your safety responsibilities don't end when you exit the water.

### Immediate Medical Assessment

After your session, check for:
- Cuts, bruises, or abrasions that need cleaning
- Signs of concussion (dizziness, nausea, confusion)
- Hypothermia symptoms (shivering, slurred speech, confusion)
- Delayed pain or stiffness
- Ear pain (surfer's ear or infection)

Don't ignore minor injuries—saltwater infections can develop quickly. Clean all wounds thoroughly with fresh water and antiseptic.

### Equipment Maintenance

Rinse all equipment with fresh water after every session:
- Wetsuit (turn inside out and hang properly)
- Surfboard (check for new dings)
- Leash (inspect for wear)
- Fins (check tightness)

Proper maintenance prevents equipment failure that could lead to dangerous situations.

## Surfing in Different Conditions

Different environments require adapted safety approaches.

### Cold Water Surfing

Cold water (below 60°F/15°C) introduces additional hazards:
- **Hypothermia**: Wear appropriate wetsuit thickness
- **Cold water shock**: Enter gradually to acclimate
- **Reduced dexterity**: Harder to grab your board or swim effectively
- **Shorter safe exposure time**: Know your limits

Cold water impairs judgment and physical ability. The **Safety Timer in One Tap Alert** is especially important for cold-water sessions, as hypothermia can set in faster than you realize, potentially leaving you unable to call for help manually.

### Reef and Point Breaks

Rocky and reef breaks present unique dangers:
- Sharp coral or rocks can cause severe lacerations
- Shallow water increases injury risk
- Uneven bottom creates unpredictable waves
- Limited exit points can trap you

Scout the break from shore first, noting safe entry and exit points. Store photos of these areas or landmark descriptions in your phone so you can reference them, and ensure **One Tap Alert** is ready if you're injured on the reef.

### Dawn and Dusk Sessions

Low-light surfing is magical but risky:
- Reduced visibility makes spotting hazards difficult
- Marine life is more active (shark feeding times)
- Fewer people around if you need help
- Harder for others to see you in distress

For dawn patrol or sunset sessions, the **One Tap Alert Safety Timer** is essential. Set it before you paddle out, and your emergency contacts will know to check on you if you don't return when expected.
