## What Is Altitude Sickness?

Altitude sickness, also known as acute mountain sickness (AMS), occurs when you ascend to high elevations too quickly, preventing your body from adjusting to lower oxygen levels. This condition typically affects travelers at elevations above 8,000 feet (2,400 meters), though some people experience symptoms at lower altitudes.

The condition ranges from mild to severe and includes three main forms:

- **Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS)** — The mildest and most common form
- **High Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE)** — Fluid buildup in the lungs, potentially life-threatening
- **High Altitude Cerebral Edema (HACE)** — Fluid buildup in the brain, a medical emergency

### Who Is at Risk?

Altitude sickness doesn't discriminate based on fitness level. Even Olympic athletes can suffer from AMS while sedentary individuals may have no issues. Risk factors include:

- Rapid ascent to high elevations
- Previous history of altitude sickness
- Living at sea level (your body isn't acclimatized)
- Certain pre-existing medical conditions
- Dehydration and alcohol consumption
- Physical exertion before acclimatization

## Recognizing Altitude Sickness Symptoms

Early recognition of altitude sickness symptoms can prevent a minor headache from becoming a life-threatening emergency. Symptoms typically appear within 6-24 hours of reaching high altitude.

### Mild Symptoms (AMS)

- Headache (often the first symptom)
- Nausea and loss of appetite
- Dizziness or lightheadedness
- Fatigue and weakness
- Difficulty sleeping
- Shortness of breath during exertion

### Severe Symptoms (HAPE/HACE)

Seek immediate medical attention if you experience:

- Severe persistent headache
- Confusion or difficulty walking
- Chest tightness and persistent cough
- Extreme fatigue and weakness
- Shortness of breath at rest
- Blue or gray lips and fingernails
- Rapid heartbeat even while resting

## Proven Altitude Sickness Prevention Strategies

Prevention is your best defense against altitude sickness. Follow these evidence-based strategies to minimize your risk:

### Ascend Gradually

The golden rule of altitude sickness prevention is "climb high, sleep low." Your body needs time to adjust to reduced oxygen levels.

**Best practices:**

- Limit elevation gain to 1,000 feet (300 meters) per day once above 8,000 feet
- Spend 2-3 nights at intermediate elevations when possible
- Include rest days every 3-4 days during multi-day ascents
- If you must ascend quickly, plan extra acclimatization days at your destination

### Stay Properly Hydrated

Dehydration accelerates altitude sickness symptoms and makes acclimatization more difficult. At high altitudes, you lose water faster through increased respiration and dry air.

**Hydration guidelines:**

- Drink 4-6 liters of water daily at high altitude
- Monitor urine color (should be pale yellow)
- Avoid excessive caffeine and alcohol, which promote dehydration
- Start hydrating 2-3 days before your trip
- Drink before you feel thirsty

### Fuel Your Body Properly

Proper nutrition supports your body's acclimatization process and maintains energy levels.

**Dietary recommendations:**

- Eat small, frequent meals rather than large portions
- Consume 70% of calories from carbohydrates (they require less oxygen to metabolize)
- Include foods rich in potassium (bananas, potatoes, dried fruit)
- Avoid heavy, fatty meals that are harder to digest
- Don't force yourself to eat if nauseous, but maintain fluid intake

### Avoid Overexertion

Physical exertion before acclimatization significantly increases altitude sickness risk.

**Activity guidelines:**

- Take it easy for the first 24-48 hours at elevation
- Reduce exercise intensity by 50% initially
- Take frequent rest breaks
- Listen to your body—exhaustion is a warning sign
- Postpone strenuous activities until you're acclimatized

## Medication and Supplementation

Several medications and supplements can help prevent altitude sickness when used appropriately.

### Acetazolamide (Diamox)

This prescription medication is the gold standard for altitude sickness prevention.

**How it works:**
- Speeds up acclimatization by increasing breathing rate
- Reduces fluid retention
- Improves oxygenation during sleep

**Typical dosing:**
- 125-250mg twice daily, starting 1-2 days before ascent
- Continue for 2-3 days at elevation or until acclimatized

**Consult your doctor** before using Diamox, especially if you have sulfa allergies or kidney problems.

### Other Prevention Options

- **Ibuprofen** — May reduce headache severity (600mg every 8 hours)
- **Dexamethasone** — Reserved for emergency treatment or prevention when rapid ascent is unavoidable
- **Ginkgo biloba** — Some evidence suggests 120-160mg twice daily may help (less reliable than Diamox)

## Special Considerations for Different Activities

### Trekking and Mountaineering

Multi-day treks present unique altitude challenges:

- Build in acclimatization days every 3-4 days
- Choose routes with gradual elevation gain
- Carry comprehensive first aid including altitude sickness medications
- Use **One Tap Alert's Safety Timer** for each day's trekking
- Never split up your group at high altitude without emergency communication plans

### Skiing and Snowboarding

Ski resorts at high altitude can affect even experienced athletes:

- Arrive 24-48 hours before skiing
- Ski at lower elevations initially
- Avoid alcohol the first night
- Stay hydrated despite cold temperatures
- Keep **One Tap Alert** accessible in an interior pocket (cold can drain phone batteries)

### High-Altitude Travel with Children

Children are equally susceptible to altitude sickness but may not communicate symptoms clearly:

- Monitor children closely for behavior changes
- Ask frequently about headaches and nausea
- Consider more conservative ascent rates
- Set up **One Tap Alert** on children's devices with parental emergency contacts
- Never separate from children at high altitude
