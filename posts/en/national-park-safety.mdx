## Understanding the Unique Risks of National Parks

National parks present a different risk profile than urban or suburban environments. Recognizing these unique hazards is the first step toward staying safe.

### Remote Locations and Limited Cell Service

Most national parks span thousands of acres of wilderness with minimal infrastructure. Cell phone coverage is often spotty or non-existent, making it difficult to call for help in an emergency. Even popular trails can have dead zones where standard communication methods fail.

### Distance from Emergency Services

In urban areas, emergency responders can typically arrive within minutes. In national parks, rescue operations may take hours or even days. Search and rescue teams must often navigate difficult terrain, and helicopter evacuations depend on weather conditions and daylight.

The faster rescuers know your exact location, the quicker they can reach you. One Tap Alert's real-time location sharing means your emergency contacts can immediately relay precise GPS coordinates to park rangers and search and rescue teams, dramatically reducing response times.

### Wildlife Encounters

From black bears in the Smokies to mountain lions in the Rockies, national parks are home to wildlife that can pose serious threats when surprised or provoked. While attacks are rare, they do happen, and knowing how to respond is critical.

### Weather Variability

Mountain weather can change in minutes. What starts as a sunny afternoon hike can turn into a dangerous thunderstorm, snowstorm, or flash flood situation. Hypothermia is a real risk even in summer months at higher elevations.

## Trail Safety: Best Practices for Hiking and Backpacking

Once you're on the trail, smart decisions and awareness keep you safe.

### Stay on Marked Trails

Wandering off established trails increases your risk of getting lost, encountering unstable terrain, and disturbing fragile ecosystems. Even if you're an experienced hiker, unfamiliar terrain can be disorienting.

Before you start each trail segment, activate **One Tap Alert's Safety Timer** with your expected completion time. This ensures that if you do become lost or disoriented, your emergency contacts will be alerted automatically.

### Pace Yourself and Know Your Limits

Many national park accidents occur when hikers overestimate their abilities or push beyond their physical limits. Start with shorter, easier trails and gradually build up to more challenging terrain. Take regular breaks, stay hydrated, and don't hesitate to turn back if conditions change or you're struggling.

### Hike with a Buddy When Possible

There's safety in numbers. A hiking partner can provide assistance if you're injured, help with navigation, and serve as an extra set of eyes for hazards. ### Carry Navigation Tools and Know How to Use Them

GPS devices and smartphone apps are excellent navigation aids, but batteries die and technology fails. Always carry a physical map and compass as backup, and know how to use them. Mark waypoints on your GPS device and take note of landmarks as you hike.

## Wildlife Safety in National Parks

Respecting wildlife isn't just about conservation—it's about your safety.

### Maintain Safe Distances

Every national park has guidelines for safe wildlife viewing distances (typically 25 yards for most animals and 100 yards for bears and wolves). Use binoculars or zoom lenses to get close-up views without approaching animals.

### Store Food Properly

Use bear canisters or park-provided food storage lockers to secure all food, toiletries, and scented items. Never keep food in your tent. Proper food storage prevents wildlife from becoming habituated to human food, which often leads to dangerous encounters.

### Know How to Respond to Wildlife Encounters

Research the specific wildlife in your destination park and understand appropriate responses. For example, making yourself appear large and making noise is appropriate for black bears but not grizzlies. If you're charged by a mountain lion, fight back; if you encounter a bear, your response depends on the species and situation.

In the frightening event of a wildlife attack or serious encounter, **One Tap Alert's instant SOS button** can summon help immediately. ## Weather Preparedness and Environmental Hazards

Nature's power demands respect and preparation.

### Monitor Changing Conditions

Weather can shift rapidly in wilderness areas. Watch for darkening clouds, sudden temperature drops, increasing wind, or other signs of changing weather. Don't hesitate to turn back or seek shelter if conditions deteriorate.

### Understand Elevation Risks

Higher elevations bring lower oxygen levels, more intense UV radiation, and colder temperatures. Altitude sickness can affect anyone, regardless of fitness level. Ascend gradually, stay hydrated, and descend immediately if you experience severe symptoms.

### Avoid Flash Flood Zones

Never camp in dry washes or narrow canyons during monsoon season or when rain is forecasted. Flash floods can occur even when it's not raining at your location—storms miles away can send walls of water down canyons with little warning.

If you find yourself in any dangerous weather situation, use **One Tap Alert** to share your real-time location with emergency contacts so they know exactly where you are if conditions worsen and you need rescue.

## Emergency Response: What to Do When Things Go Wrong

Despite best efforts, emergencies happen. Knowing how to respond can save your life.

### Stay Calm and Assess the Situation

Panic leads to poor decisions. If you're lost, injured, or facing another emergency, stop moving, take deep breaths, and carefully evaluate your options. Sit down, drink water, and think through your situation logically.

### Signal for Help

If you need rescue, use **One Tap Alert's SOS button** immediately to alert your emergency contacts with your exact GPS location. Additionally, use traditional signaling methods: three of anything (whistle blows, mirror flashes, fires) is the universal distress signal. Stay in one location once you've signaled for help—moving makes it harder for rescuers to find you.

### Provide First Aid

If you or someone in your group is injured, provide appropriate first aid within your skill level. Stop bleeding, immobilize fractures, and treat for shock. This is where having uploaded medical information to One Tap Alert's Secure Vault becomes crucial—emergency responders can access critical health information even if you're unconscious.

### Prepare to Spend the Night

If darkness is falling or weather is worsening, don't try to push through to your destination. Use your emergency shelter, stay dry, and conserve energy. Survival often comes down to staying put and maintaining core body temperature until rescue arrives.

Your emergency contacts monitoring your location through **One Tap Alert** can inform rescuers of your last known position, significantly improving the chances of a quick rescue even if you can't actively communicate.
